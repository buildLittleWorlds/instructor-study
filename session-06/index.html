The Instructor's Study · Session 6

Knowing Without Reading

Dr. Plate — AI + Research, Level 2 · Youth Horizons Learning
Thesis. You are publishing apps whose code you have not read, and you know they work because you clicked them. That is a real way of knowing, it is not only happening in code, and it is not entirely new. What is new is that the layer you stopped reading can be confidently wrong — and that some layers cannot be read at all.

Course boundary: this is an instructor demonstration. Your choices stay in this browser tab; nothing is collected, exported, or submitted.

This week's question: how do you know what your app does?

Three rungs

Three things you could try to understand. Each one gives you two ways in. Press both on all three and notice where one of the doors stops working.

Pick a rung, then press read it or check it.

The third rung is the one to sit with. On rung one both doors work. On rung two both doors still work — nobody read that code, but somebody could, so sometimes you should. On rung three read it stops being a door at all. You can look at the numbers for as long as you like and learn nothing about what the thing will do next. Researchers do study what is inside these models, and that work is real and unfinished — but it is not reading, and it does not work the way reading code works.

Honesty label: the numbers on rung three are illustrative, generated on this page. They are not weights pulled out of a real model. The point they make — that a grid of numbers tells you nothing about behavior — is exactly as true of the real ones, which is why an illustration is honest here.

The confident summary

Here is a small app, all of it, and a description of what it does. The description reads like something an assistant would hand you. Four of its five claims are true of this code. One is not.

Find the wrong one before you press anything. Then check each claim and see how you did.

quiz.js — the whole thing
const questions = ["Is a tomato a fruit?",
                   "Do fish sleep?",
                   "Is glass a liquid?"];
const answers   = [true, true, false];

let score   = 0;
let current = 0;

function ask() {
  document.getElementById("q").textContent = questions[current];
}

function answer(choice) {
  if (choice === answers[current]) {
    score = score + 1;
  }
  current = current + 1;
  if (current < questions.length) {
    ask();
  } else {
    document.getElementById("q").textContent = "Done! Score: " + score;
  }
}

document.getElementById("yes").onclick = () => answer(true);
document.getElementById("no").onclick  = () => answer(false);
ask();
What the description says it does

0 of 5 checked.

That is what confidently wrong looks like. The false claim is not nonsense. It is the reasonable thing — saving a best score is what a quiz app usually does — stated in exactly the same voice as the four true ones. Nothing in the description marks it as the shaky one. A calculator cannot do that to you. It gives you an answer or an error, never a confident invention.

Honesty label: this description was written by hand to demonstrate the failure mode; it is not the live output of a model. A real assistant summarising twenty-four lines would very likely get all five right. That is the actual problem — you cannot tell the accurate summary from the confident one by looking at it, which is why the only move is to check.

Where else this happened

Not only code, and not only now. Pick a field.

Four of these six happened before AI. That is why they are here. If this page told you the situation was unprecedented, you would find a counterexample in about four minutes and stop believing the rest of it. People have been handing work to tools for a very long time, and every time it happened somebody asked whether it was really still the same craft. The contrast is not a hedge — it is the only way to see what actually is different this time.

So what is actually new?

Three things, and only three. Everything else on this page is old news wearing new clothes.

A calculator can't lie to you. Every older tool on that list was reliable underneath. A spreadsheet computes exactly what you told it, even when what you told it was nonsense. This is the first one that can invent something and sound exactly as confident as when it is right.
Some things can't be read at all. Code AI wrote is unread but readable — somebody could. A model is different in kind, not in size. That is rung three, and it is why checking is not a convenience there.
You can do work whose nuts and bolts you don't know. Not "software got easier." Who can do what changed. A teacher hands out a plan they did not write; a scientist summarises studies they did not read; you publish an app you cannot read. This is the one that is about you.

So if you did not read it, and some of it cannot be read, and what you did not read can be confidently wrong — then checking is not a chore you do after the real work. It is the only door there is. The rest of this page is about how you build one, and it is next session's subject.

A window, not a verdict

A good check lets you learn something real about a system without inspecting all of it. That matters for apps and for AI models, where reading every line, internal state, model weight, or possible use is not realistic. The check does not make the whole system transparent. It makes one chosen behavior observable under named conditions.

Three meanings of "it works"

A window shows one behavior — so which behavior you choose is the whole game. Below is Bill Gosper's glider gun, the 1970 machine that answered Conway's $50 wager by firing a fresh glider every 30 generations, forever. Watch it run, then put the same system through three different meanings of "it works."

Generation 0 · the gun waits.

1 · Reach the target onceDoes a glider cross into the far field within 50 generations?
2 · Keep workingIs it still firing around generation 150?
3 · Recover from damageDamage the board first, then: is it still firing long after your hit?

All three verdicts are computed by silently running the real board and checking the far field (the lower-right region a fired glider must cross). Tests 1 and 2 restart from the intact gun; test 3 runs from whatever you left on the board. The board is finite; gliders that reach the edge are absorbed — which is exactly why "still firing long after the hit" is the honest recovery check: it waits out the gliders that were already in flight when you struck.

Same system, three verdicts. The gun almost always passes 1 and 2 and fails 3 — one clumsy thumbprint and the factory unravels, because nothing ever trained it to survive you. The Growing Neural Cellular Automata paper makes exactly this move at training time: models trained only to grow may decay or explode; trained to persist, they hold; trained with damage, they regrow limbs they never lost before. Robustness isn't a property systems have by default — it's a property something put there. Watch the trained versions recover: Distill 2020 · the 2026 sequel.

The yardstick makes the jump

One more honesty tool, one research argument up from the rubric: sometimes the instrument manufactures the story. A famous debate about language models asks whether abilities "emerge" suddenly as models grow. The mirage argument: score with an all-or-nothing yardstick and ability seems to snap into existence; score the same system with partial credit and it grows smoothly. Here is that whole argument in one slider — ten-digit addition, the debate's own example.

Partial credit (average digits right, p):

Where the "jump" lives. The skill line is smooth by construction — nothing in the system ever jumps. Only the all-or-nothing curve has a cliff, and the cliff is pure arithmetic: p¹⁰ stays near zero until p is large, then soars. Whether an ability "emerges" can depend on the instrument (Schaeffer, Miranda & Koyejo 2023, answering Wei et al. 2022 — and the argument is live, not settled). Your four criteria above are instruments too: that is why this page shows you your least-settled criterion instead of pretending the rubric is a ruler of atoms.

What's computed: both curves are exact arithmetic from the slider (nothing simulated); the graded sum below the slider is a real random draw at your chosen skill — reroll it and watch all-or-nothing swing while partial credit barely moves.

Method

Loading…

The gun bench. Real bounded Conway board (60×40), the standard Gosper gun. Reach = any live cell in the far field (the region a fired glider must cross) within 50 generations of the intact gun; keep-working = far field still occupied when sampled around generation 140–160; recover = far field still occupied 140–200 generations after your damage, from the damaged board — late enough that gliders already in flight have been absorbed and only a working gun could still be supplying new ones.

The yardstick. All-or-nothing = p¹⁰; partial credit = p. The sample sum draws ten digits independently right with probability p. The thresholds and the framing are mine; the arithmetic is the arithmetic.

The ladder. Rungs one and two are real, runnable code — the second is a genuinely over-built counter, which is the point. Rung three's numbers are generated on this page from a fixed seed, so the grid is the same every visit; they are an illustration, not weights extracted from a model. Nothing about the illustration softens the claim, because a grid of real weights would be exactly as unreadable.

The confident summary. The five claims were written by hand against the code shown beside them, with one made false on purpose. Four are checkable line by line in the twenty-four lines displayed; the false one is checkable too, by its absence — nothing in the file saves anything.

Interpretation

Every rung on that ladder is a way of knowing something you did not make. Two of them let you read; all three of them let you check. That is the whole session, and it is why the second half of this page exists — if reading is not available, the check is not a nicety, it is the access route, and a bad one leaves you knowing nothing while feeling informed.

The side exhibit adds the other half of the same problem. Same openings, same raters — the only thing that changed between the rounds was having words for what we were looking for. On gut feeling, the panel scattered from 1 to 5 on the same sentence; with the rubric, they converged. That convergence is what lets a judgment about writing count as evidence instead of just a vibe. And notice the second gift: the trained panel didn't just agree, it agreed on different scores for different openings — low for the cliché, high for the pigeon — so the criteria don't flatten everything, they discriminate. This is exactly how real AI systems are evaluated: break the fuzzy judgment into named criteria, rate blind, and check that independent judges land in the same place.

Limitation. Rating the same items twice means Round 2 is nudged by having already seen them — a cleaner test would use two separate trained-and-untrained groups on fresh items (which is what the real panels here do). And "agreement" isn't "correct": a whole panel can agree and still be wrong. Agreement makes a judgment usable as evidence, not automatically true.

Try it yourself

Ask an AI to explain your own app to you. Read what it says. Then pick one claim out of it and check that one thing on your real public page. Finish: “The AI told me my app ___. When I checked, ___.”

Pick something checkable. "It says there's a reset button" can be checked. "It says it's a fun game" cannot — pick a different one.

"It was right" is a real result and it counts fully. You used the non-reading route on purpose and confirmed a piece of it, which is the entire skill.

And when the summary turns out to be wrong — when the reset button it promised is not there — the next skill is the loop that fixes it. That is next session's bench.

Authorship note

What AI helped with: drafting this page's code, the agreement math, and the over-built counter on rung two — all of which I checked. The story openings are a mix I chose; the panels are illustrative ratings.

What I own: the three rungs and the choice to give each one two doors rather than describing them; the decision to make exactly one summary claim false and to make it the most plausible one; the six fields and the choice to lead with four that predate AI; the four criteria and their scale anchors; and the honesty that agreement is not the same as being right.

What I got wrong once, in public: I asked an AI to build something with Hugging Face models. It did, and it also spent money I had not approved, because I was not reading the layer where that decision got made. I was the author and I did not know. That is why nothing built in this course has an account, a key, or anything that can spend.
