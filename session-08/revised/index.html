Why the image case was replaced

Unlike the original Version 2, whose motivating scenario was a labeled simulation, this revision is driven by an actual, dated observation. No student is quoted or identified.

Observation

Walking the class through the Sessions 7–10 arc (2026-07-28), the pond image read as flat beside the moving glider. Its four memberships were asserted by buttons more than perceived in the picture; nothing in the image behaved.

Interpretation

The medium fought the question. A still image can only tell plural membership; it cannot perform it. The glider’s whole point is identity through change in time — the companion case needed time inside it.

Revision

Replace the image with a short musical figure, synthesized in the browser. Notes are cells on a grid; chords, lines, and the build are wholes; the ear does the grouping. The glider, the recurring action, and the notebook all stay.

The replacement medium came from rewatching the music-shop scene in Once (2007): one musician teaches the other the song by isolating its parts and then combining them — the chords played alone, the lead voice joining, a second voice entering above it, the room swelling — filmed in long takes with only a handful of cuts while the camera slowly pushes in. The film stages the arrangement’s own architecture. The Play the build button below is that scene turned into an interaction. A guitar teacher’s close reading of a live duet performance, published this same week, supplied the scale-degree analysis the motif cells below use — and the observation that in that session the voices move largely in unison, which the second-voice toggle lets you hear for yourself. The song itself lives on YouTube — this page deliberately contains none of it.

The two cases, side by side

The glider and the motif

Both cases put the same question in front of a reader: a whole made of parts that change. The analogy is useful exactly as far as it holds — and instructive where it breaks.

Where the analogy holds

  • Both live on a grid of discrete parts. Cells on the board; notes on the roll. Each part is either there or not.
  • Both stay recognizable while their parts change. The glider’s five cells are replaced as it travels; the verse cell returns as the chorus cell with its turn flipped (3-2 → 7-1), and the ear still says same thing.
  • Both wholes are drawn by an observer. The B3/S23 rule never mentions a glider; the air never carries a motif. Grouping is something a reader or listener does — which is why the Boundary Notebook asks for your rule.
  • One part can serve several wholes at once. A note is simultaneously in a chord (vertical), a line (horizontal), and the figure (a window of the grid). That is the droplet’s question, now audible.

Where the analogy breaks

  • Rule versus choice. Life’s next generation is forced by one law; nothing forces the next note. The glider’s changed cells are consequences; the chorus’s changed cells were decisions.
  • Local versus contextual meaning. A cell’s fate depends only on its eight neighbors. A note’s role depends on everything sounding with and around it — the same C is the root of the C chord and, a bar later, the fifth of F.
  • Nobody versus somebody. No one chooses the glider’s next cells. Every note here was chosen — first by the songwriters, then, for these synthesized lines, by the instructor. Whose name goes on a whole is Session 10’s question.
  • Doubling has no Life analogue. Two voices can occupy the same line — in unison, or a third apart — and the whole thickens without adding any new cells to the pattern. The cited live-session reading locates the duet’s power in exactly that unison, two people breathing one line.

The course’s standing rule applies: analogies find questions, not answers. Nothing above claims the song computes, or that the board sings.

Seed · planted 2026-08-01 · to be grown in a later version

The word for untying

The oldest name for what this page does is Greek. ἀνάλυσις (análysis) comes from ἀναλύειν (analýein), “to unloose, to untie” — ἀνά (“up, throughout”) plus λύσις (“a loosening, a setting free”). Before it meant breaking a problem into components, it meant untying a knot. Its old companion is σύνθεσις (sýnthesis), “a placing together.”

The bench above does both, in that order. The solo buttons are ἀνάλυσις: they untie the figure and set each strand free to be examined alone. The build is σύνθεσις: it places the strands back together until they are one thing again. The music-shop scene is a lesson conducted in exactly this grammar — untie the song, hand someone one strand, then bind it again with a second pair of hands.

Whether the untied strands or the retied knot is the real thing — whether a whole survives its own analysis — is this page’s open question wearing older clothes. Left here to grow.

Paper layer · rewritten in the revision

A figure can participate in more than one organized whole

Question

When a handful of notes recurs — played alone, layered under a line, flipped into a chorus, swelled and stripped back — what keeps it one thing? And is its unity in the notes, in their relations, or in the listener grouping them?

Observation

The page directly shows that the same synthesized notes can be heard alone and inside larger wholes without changing pitch or duration; that one selected note can hold several simultaneous memberships; that one changed cell turns the verse cell into the chorus cell; and that the glider still verifies its counts one section above.

Interpretation

The glider persists by propagation: a rule forces the next cells. The motif persists by repetition and relation: nothing forces its return — an author chose it, and a listener re-binds it. Both boundaries are defensible, but the constraint behind each is different in kind.

Limitation

The audio here is an original synthesis of chords and analyzed figures — an analysis, not the song. The pre-chorus line is a course approximation, and one song settles nothing about musical unity in general. The two cited sources describe different performances, and they differ (thirds versus unison); the toggle presents both rather than deciding.

Current claim · Version 2, revised

A part can belong to several wholes when each membership names a defensible relation — and in music the relations are audible: simultaneity makes a chord, succession makes a line, recurrence makes a motif, and doubling makes one line into a duet.
