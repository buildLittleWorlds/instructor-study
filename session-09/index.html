The paper · abstract

A glider in Conway’s Game of Life keeps its name while every cell composing it is replaced. A two-bar musical figure from “Falling Slowly” (Hansard & Irglová, from Once, 2007) keeps its identity while its notes are silenced, nudged, doubled, and renamed. This page asks what the two survivals share. Using one verified automaton and one browser-synthesized figure, it follows a line from von Ehrenfels’s transposed melodies [1] and Dennett’s gliders [2] into current research on polysemantic neurons, sparse feature dictionaries, and circuit tracing [7] [10]. It argues that a whole is not a roster of parts but a relation the parts are caught in—and that the parts easiest to point to may not be the parts that best explain the whole. Every figure in this paper is playable; every claim is labeled directly shown, source-supported, or still open.

Keywords: parts and wholes (mereology) · Gestalt qualities · auditory scene analysis · cellular automata · motif · mechanistic interpretability · polysemanticity · monosemantic features

The paper · §1

The problem of the one

In 1890, Christian von Ehrenfels opened the founding paper of Gestalt psychology with a puzzle about melody: transpose a tune and every note changes, yet the melody survives [1]. Whatever a melody is, it is not the notes — it is something the notes are doing. The puzzle is older than psychology: Plutarch’s Athenians replaced the Ship of Theseus plank by plank and argued about whether it stayed the same ship [3]. The glider below — introduced to the world in Martin Gardner’s October 1970 column [4] — runs both puzzles at machine speed: advance it one generation and the counts read three survived, two appeared, two disappeared, yet the name holds. Whether that name reports something real or merely convenient is a live question in philosophy [2]. If a whole can survive the replacement of its parts, the whole is a relation, not a roster. The cases below put that claim where it can be tested.

The paper · §2

The unmoved part

Hearing is not a microphone. Albert Bregman’s Auditory Scene Analysis describes the ear as an organizer that sorts arriving sound into streams and objects, deciding what belongs with what before you are aware a decision was made [5]. One consequence: a note’s character is not carried in the note. The same C is restful as the root of a C chord and suspended, leaning, as a fourth against G — the performance analysis this page cites names the move in passing: when the harmony shifts, this C becomes the fifth of F. If character lived in the note, changing its surroundings should change nothing. If it lives in the relation, then holding the part perfectly fixed while the whole moves around it should transform what you hear. The experiment below holds the C. You judge what moves.

The paper · §3

What the whole survives

Daniel Dennett’s test for whether a pattern is real is compression: a pattern earns its name when describing it is shorter than listing its parts [2]. A corollary follows — parts differ in how much of the description they carry. Silence one note of the arpeggio and the figure shrugs; its description barely changes. Silence the turn of the verse cell and the description collapses. Hobbes sharpened the ship puzzle by rebuilding a second ship from the discarded planks [3]; the lab below runs the milder experiment — change parts one at a time and listen for the moment the whole stops answering to its name. Some parts are scaffolding and some are keystones, and no rule on this page will say which is which. The only instrument is the ear. Break it and listen.

The paper · §4

Where the joints are

In 1983, Fred Lerdahl and Ray Jackendoff attempted something audacious: to write down, explicitly, the rules a listener uses to carve music into groups — a grammar of segmentation, a B3/S23 for hearing [6]. Their preference rules formalize what Bregman’s ear does tacitly [5]. Written rules invite a test this page can actually run. The build below plays with its narration hidden: no verse, no chorus, no names at all. You mark every joint you hear; only afterward do the names arrive, and the two maps argue. Where they agree, the names look like descriptions. Where they disagree, the names look like decisions — the question the Trailheads card asked. The theory claims your ear runs rules it never wrote down. Map first. Name after.

    The two cases, side by side

    The glider and the motif

    Both cases put the same question in front of a reader: a whole made of parts that change. The analogy is useful exactly as far as it holds — and instructive where it breaks.

    Where the analogy holds

    • Both live on a grid of discrete parts. Cells on the board; notes on the roll. Each part is either there or not.
    • Both stay recognizable while their parts change. The glider’s five cells are replaced as it travels; the verse cell returns as the chorus cell with its turn flipped (3-2 → 7-1), and the ear still says same thing.
    • Both wholes are drawn by an observer. The B3/S23 rule never mentions a glider; the air never carries a motif. Grouping is something a reader or listener does — which is why the Boundary Notebook asks for your rule.
    • One part can serve several wholes at once. The held C completes the C chord, the F chord, the A-minor chord, and the G-major tension in turn — same pitch, four memberships, all audible.

    Where the analogy breaks

    • Rule versus choice. Life’s next generation is forced by one law; nothing forces the next note. The glider’s changed cells are consequences; the chorus’s changed cells were decisions.
    • Local versus contextual meaning. A cell’s fate depends only on its eight neighbors. A note’s role depends on everything sounding with and around it — the unmoved note above is the proof you can hold.
    • Nobody versus somebody. No one chooses the glider’s next cells. Every note here was chosen — first by the songwriters, then, for these synthesized lines, by the instructor. Whose name goes on a whole is Session 10’s question.
    • Fragile versus resilient unity. Perturb the glider and it usually disintegrates; perturb the figure and it often survives — and one of its mutations is the chorus. Break-it-and-listen makes the difference an experiment, not a claim.

    The course’s standing rule applies: analogies find questions, not answers. Nothing above claims the song computes, or that the board sings.

    The word for untying

    The oldest name for what this page does is Greek. ἀνάλυσις (análysis) comes from ἀναλύειν (analýein), “to unloose, to untie” — ἀνά (“up, throughout”) plus λύσις (“a loosening, a setting free”). Before it meant breaking a problem into components, it meant untying a knot. Its old companion is σύνθεσις (sýnthesis), “a placing together.”

    The bench above does both, in that order. The solo buttons are ἀνάλυσις: they untie the figure and set each strand free to be examined alone. The build is σύνθεσις: it places the strands back together until they are one thing again. The music-shop scene is a lesson conducted in exactly this grammar — untie the song, hand someone one strand, then bind it again with a second pair of hands.

    Whether the untied strands or the retied knot is the real thing — whether a whole survives its own analysis — is this page’s open question wearing older clothes. Left here to grow.

    Experimental paper extension · a current research bridge

    Is the unit we can point to the unit doing the work?

    Von Ehrenfels asks how one melody can survive a change in every note. Mechanistic interpretability asks an almost reversed question: when a model produces one behavior, what internal parts made it happen—and are neurons even the right parts to name? Anthropic’s toy models show how a network can pack more sparse features than it has dimensions, so one neuron may participate in several apparently unrelated features. They call the resulting mixed behavior polysemanticity and the packing scheme superposition [7]. The visible implementation unit can therefore be a poor explanatory unit.

    That makes the held C a useful question-generator, with an important boundary. The note is one observable part whose musical role changes with its harmonic neighborhood. A polysemantic neuron is not a note and does not literally hear several chords; it is a coordinate in a learned computation that may carry contributions from multiple features. The analogy suggests where to look—at relations, contexts, and interventions—but it supplies no evidence about a model.

    Polysemanticity

    A single neuron can respond as though several features have been crowded into it. The research hypothesis is not simply that the neuron “has several meanings,” but that sparse features can be represented as different directions sharing limited representational space [7].

    Toward monosemanticity

    Sparse autoencoders learn a larger dictionary of feature directions from model activations. Anthropic found many learned features that were more interpretable than individual neurons and could sometimes be used to steer generation [8]. “Monosemantic” here means relatively more specific—not a guarantee that researchers have discovered the model’s one true vocabulary.

    Resolution changes the map

    When the learned dictionary gets larger, one apparently coherent feature can split into a family of narrower features. Anthropic reproduced interpretable features at Claude 3 Sonnet scale, but also found feature splitting and multiple useful resolutions [9]. The question “what is the feature?” therefore resembles this page’s map-first problem: a label can disclose a pattern and still depend on the granularity of the map.

    From labels to circuits

    A feature that activates is not yet an explanation. Later circuit-tracing work connects features into partial causal graphs and intervenes on intermediate states to test whether they influence the output [10]. The musical analogue is not merely naming a motif; it is holding, silencing, or nudging a part and listening for what changes.

    One representation, several uses

    Anthropic’s July 2026 “global workspace” research identifies a small J-space of internal patterns that can be reported, manipulated, and reused by different downstream tasks. Replacing one country pattern changed answers about capital, language, continent, and currency, while other automatic processing bypassed the space [11]. This is a striking new version of one part participating in several wholes—but it is evidence about a particular model and method, not evidence of consciousness or a universal model architecture.

    What this app directly shows
    One pitch can retain its frequency while its audible role changes; one motif can survive some interventions and not others; readers can draw different boundary maps.
    What the research supports
    Polysemantic neurons and superposition in toy models; relatively interpretable sparse-autoencoder features; feature splitting across dictionary sizes; partial causal graphs; and, in Anthropic’s 2026 experiments, a small reusable J-space with causally tested effects [7]–[11].
    What remains open
    Whether musical grouping offers more than a teaching analogy for model decomposition; which learned feature dictionaries best reflect a model’s computation; how much any current map leaves out; and how broadly the J-space result generalizes.
    Paper experiment · no model-internals claim

    Treat every label as a provisional feature dictionary

    Decompose: name the smallest musical roles you think the page needs—root, fifth, tension, verse cell, chorus turn. Probe: play each role across several contexts and record where the label predicts what you hear. Intervene: hold, silence, or nudge the candidate feature. Split: when one label hides two different behaviors, replace it with two narrower labels. Then ask whether the finer dictionary explains more while still leaving the whole recognizable.

    This is an interpretability-inspired analysis of the app, not mechanistic interpretability performed on a language model. Its scholarly value lies in making decomposition, intervention, granularity, and uncertainty explicit enough to compare with the cited research.

    Paper layer · rewritten in the second revision

    A figure can participate in more than one organized whole

    Question

    When a handful of notes recurs — played alone, layered under a line, flipped into a chorus, swelled and stripped back — what keeps it one thing? And when the whole falls into sections, do names like chorus describe joints the ear finds, or decide them?

    Observation

    The page directly shows that a held C changes character as the chords change around it while its frequency does not move; that silencing or nudging single notes leaves the figure sometimes intact and sometimes broken — and that one particular nudge lands on the chorus’s own cells; and that the glider still verifies its counts one section above.

    Interpretation

    The glider persists by propagation: a rule forces the next cells. The motif persists by repetition and relation: nothing forces its return — an author chose it, and a listener re-binds it. What a part is — root, fifth, third, tension — is decided by the whole that is sounding, not by the part alone.

    Limitation

    The audio here is an original synthesis of chords and analyzed figures — an analysis, not the song. The pre-chorus line is a course approximation. The labs report facts and replay sound; the verdicts — did the whole survive? did the note change? — belong to each reader’s ear, and ears differ. One song settles nothing about musical unity in general.

    Current claim · Version 3, interpretability extension

    A part can belong to several wholes—and the unit easiest to point to may not be the unit that best explains the whole. Names are provisional decompositions: map, intervene, compare resolutions, and state what the map leaves out.
