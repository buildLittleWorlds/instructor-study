Essay · Game of Life beside John Cage

    
The glider does not compose alone.

    
Conway’s Game of Life can generate a perfectly exact succession of states without generating a single note. John Cage’s writing helps expose the gap. A musical process is not only a source of events; it is also a way of deciding what may count as sound, which decisions belong to the composer, and what must remain available to contingency.


    
Cage was asking about electronic sound before the synthesizer became ordinary.

    
In The Future of Music: Credo, the early manifesto later collected in Silence, Cage argued that electrical instruments could open composition to noise and to the whole field of audible sound. His 1990 autobiographical statement recalls an early Imaginary Landscape practice that mixed acoustic sounds, amplified small sounds, and recordings of sine waves. The comparison with this browser instrument is therefore more specific than “Cage liked randomness.” Cage repeatedly treated technology as a way to reorganize the relation among sound, score, performer, and environment.


    
Chance is a discipline, not an “anything goes” effect.

    
A useful distinction appears in the Percussive Arts Society’s account of Cage’s practice: chance operations changed his responsibility from choosing preferred answers to constructing questions whose answers he would accept.

    
“making my responsibility not the making of choices, but the asking of questions.”John Cage, quoted in the Percussive Arts Society Hall of Fame biography

    
Prepared chance in this app follows that structure in a small, transparent way. The program asks for sixteen pitch offsets within the depth chosen by the reader. The browser answers once. Those offsets then repeat, so the result can be replayed and compared. Pressing Prepare another chance score asks again. This is not a simulation of Cage’s I Ching practice, and it does not claim to compose “in Cage’s style.” It borrows one procedural insight: decide the field of possibilities, let a chance operation answer inside that field, and then attend to the result.


    
Experimental music offers several answers to “who composes?”

    
Cage’s Music of Changes used I Ching-derived operations during composition, but its resulting notation and performance were fixed in detail. Earle Brown’s open-form instructions moved different decisions into performance: composed units could be ordered, juxtaposed, paced, and shaped anew. Iannis Xenakis used probability, set theory, game theory, and computers to organize sonic masses. These are not interchangeable versions of “random music.” They locate rule, contingency, and agency in different places.

    
George Lewis pushes the comparison from generation toward interaction. His Voyager lineage treats the computer as an improvising participant in concert with human musicians, not merely a device that prints a predetermined score. Pauline Oliveros shifts attention again: Deep Listening distinguishes involuntary hearing from conscious listening and includes environment, movement, imagination, collaboration, and discussion. A finished WAV is therefore only one realization of this project. The listening situation and the decisions musicians make around it remain part of the work.


    
Life, modulation, and selected Cage works distribute authorship differently.

    
The Game of Life portion is deterministic. Given the same finite board and seed, B3/S23 produces the same next generation. The synthesizer portion is interpretive. Direct map makes row audible as pitch. Modulation wave adds a human-designed periodic contour. Prepared chance chooses a contour once and holds it stable. None is more “inside” the glider than the others. Each is a way of reading the same cellular history.

Cage’s electronics also keep this app from congratulating itself too quickly.

    
Imaginary Landscape No. 4 used twelve radios; No. 5 assembled fragments from forty-two recordings, according to the John Cage Trust’s account of the series. Later, Cage and Lejaren Hiller’s HPSCHD surrounded seven amplified harpsichords with fifty-two tapes of computer-generated sound in an event where the audience could move through the space; the University of Illinois archive preserves the tapes and production history.

    
This app is much more closed. It listens to no room, radio, microphone, or network. Its oscillators are generated locally, its pitch collection is fixed, and its “chance” is bounded by code the reader can inspect. That difference matters. The Cage comparison does not convert every algorithm into experimental music. It asks whether the system gives up enough control to let its maker hear something not already contained in a preferred melodic plan. Cage’s late statement asks listeners to attend to vibration “not necessarily two times the same.” Here the Life states can be the same while their pitch contour changes. The experiment relocates sameness rather than eliminating it.


    
The multitrack studio is a score-making system, not an autonomous composer.

    
The studio lets a musician combine independent Life seeds, offset their entrances, select duration and register, choose a scale and oscillator, and decide whether location, shape, a designed wave, or a seeded chance contour controls pitch. The exported project records every one of those decisions. That ledger matters because procedural music can hide authorship behind a rule: someone still selects the rule, the range, the timbre, the stopping point, and the conditions under which chance is allowed to answer.

    
Compared with Brown, the exported WAV freezes one realization while the JSON project keeps the units mobile. Compared with Xenakis, this app uses a deterministic cellular automaton and small chance contours rather than probability as its primary model of sonic mass. Compared with Lewis, it does not listen or improvise with a performer. Compared with Oliveros, it cannot supply the listening practice, room, body, or community around the sound. Those limits are invitations for musicians: export a realization, reopen its score, perform around it, or treat its pattern ledger as instructions for another ensemble.
