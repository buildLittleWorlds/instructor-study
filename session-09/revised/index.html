Three claims, not one conclusion

What if persistence is a way of being generated?

The two encounters make one possibility vivid: something can recur without preserving the same material. They do not prove how human memory works. The sources below turn that analogy into a disagreement worth keeping open.

1 · Dennett: real patterns

For Daniel Dennett, a glider is real because the higher-level description captures and predicts regularity more efficiently than a cell-by-cell inventory. His larger target is belief: a belief need not be a sentence stored in a particular place to be real. It can be a stable, predictive pattern in how a person responds.

Question: Is “the motif” similarly real because it lets us anticipate recurrence across changing settings?

2 · Barenholtz: generated now

Elan Barenholtz advances a stronger proposal. What feels like retrieving a finished memory or belief may instead be a new continuation generated from present context and parameters shaped by experience. Stability would then mean reliably generating related answers—not reopening the same internal file.

Question: When the cue changes, how much change can a generated memory survive before it is no longer the same memory?

3 · Spens and Burgess: generation plus traces

A peer-reviewed computational model agrees that recall is generative but retains rapid hippocampal encoding and replay. Generative schemas gradually reconstruct predictable structure while unusual details may still depend on stored traces. “Reconstructed” therefore does not automatically mean “nothing stored.”

Question: Is the better contrast storage versus generation, or several kinds of constraint working together?

Language is the bridge—and the caution

Barenholtz calls language autogenerative: its statistical organization makes coherent continuation learnable without an externally supplied symbolic rulebook. His July 2026 preprint reports that the contribution of ordered prior context to later words decays approximately as 1/d across ten corpora and six language families. That is evidence about a regularity in language. The paper explicitly says it characterizes the product, not the cognitive process that produced it. It therefore motivates the memory hypothesis without establishing it.

A short shelf

Five sources that change the question

  • Daniel Dennett, “Real Patterns” (1991) — the glider, compression, prediction, and the reality of beliefs.
  • Elan Barenholtz, “Memory Isn’t Real (Part 1)” (2025) — the accessible statement of the just-in-time generation hypothesis.
  • Barenholtz, “Autogeneration” (2025) — the proposed bridge from language’s internal statistical structure to cognition.
  • Barenholtz, “A scaling law of contextual persistence in human language” (2026 preprint) — a new quantitative result about ordered context, presented here as preprint evidence about language rather than settled evidence about memory.
  • Eleanor Spens and Neil Burgess, “A generative model of memory construction and consolidation” (2024) — an open, peer-reviewed model that makes recall generative without discarding hippocampal encoding and replay.
